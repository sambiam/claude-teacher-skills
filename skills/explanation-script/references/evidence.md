# Evidence behind the script design

Read this when you need to justify a design choice — to a faculty, in a PD
session, or to yourself when a rule in SKILL.md feels arbitrary. Nothing here
is needed to run the skill.

Each entry: what the source establishes, and precisely what it licenses in the
script.

---

## Engelmann — faultless communication

*Theory of Instruction* (Engelmann & Carnine, 1982). Summaries:
[Conception of the Good](http://conceptionofthegood.co.uk/?p=532),
[What Direct Instruction is](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/MNSgW5j8bRvynSZHR/what-direct-instruction-is).

**Establishes.** A communication is faultless when only one rule can account
for all the examples presented. Learners generalise on the basis of *sameness*
— and they extract whatever stayed the same, including features the teacher
never intended to teach (the classic case: calculus students thrown by an
integral in *t* rather than *x*, having learned an incidental rule). The
**wording principle** requires identical wording for the identical idea across
every example; rephrasing introduces a second problem to solve. Negative
examples exist to disconfirm the wrong hypotheses, and a good negative is one a
reasonable student would call positive.

**Licenses.** Step 1 in full — if the routine's decision can be skipped and the
answer still comes out right, the example is compatible with two rules and no
wording repairs that. Step 2's say/never-say table and the ban on reusing a
word that already has a referent. Step 3's fixed board positions (position is a
sameness the student will read). Step 4's "say the distractor negatively".

**Caveat worth knowing.** The strong form — "if a faultless communication
fails, the fault is the learner's" — does not survive contact with a classroom.
The workable version is diagnostic: a failure means the sequence admitted an
interpretation you did not intend, and the wrong answers tell you which. That
is why Step 7 exists.

## Barton — the "I do"

[Tips for Teachers — I Do](https://tipsforteachers.co.uk/i-do/).

**Establishes.** Three clarity failures to eliminate: **vagueness** ("kind of",
"pretty much"), **mazes** ("um", "so", "okay"), **discontinuity** (digressing
mid-explanation). Explanations should be planned in advance as bullets,
rehearsed aloud, read from a script initially. Model live rather than revealing
a finished solution. Focus attention physically ("empty hands, eyes on me") and
visually (hide the You do). Don't talk over novel text — either remove the text
and speak, or display it silently to be read. Roughly **thirty seconds** of
teacher talk before attention wanes. Check for listening with question-first,
name-second, and with repeat-backs.

**Licenses.** The whole premise that a script is worth writing. Step 4's
banned-words list, the twelve-to-fifteen statement budget, the ▶ repeat-back
marks, and the numbered-statement format over prose.

## Barton — Silent Teacher

[Tips for Teachers — Silent Teacher](https://tipsforteachers.co.uk/silent-teacher-top-5s/).

**Establishes.** Writing and talking while students watch, listen and take
notes is a four-way demand. Working the example in silence with gestures
removes one channel; students then self-explain — *what just happened*
(principled) and *what comes next* (predictive) — and record what they didn't
follow. Tips: say why you are doing it, gesture deliberately, pause between
lines, collect written questions.

**Licenses.** The Silent Teacher variant in Step 4, and the reason it is
specified as the *first* pass rather than an optional flourish: it converts
your explanation into their explanation, which is the version you can assess.

## Catrambone; Atkinson, Derry, Renkl & Wortham — subgoal labelling

[Learning from Examples](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543070002181);
[Subgoal labeling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subgoal_labeling).

**Establishes.** Naming a *group* of steps by the function it serves — rather
than narrating each step — reduces the number of things being tracked, improves
transfer to novel problems, and shows up in learners' own explanations
afterwards, indicating they internalised the structure rather than the
sequence. Labels should describe purpose, not action.

**Licenses.** Step 3's "at most three subgoals, named by function", the
insistence on breaking a formula across lines at the subgoal boundaries, and
the distinction between a subgoal ("area of the cross-section") and a step
("write the units"). Also the argument against the five-numbered-steps list
that decks tend to accumulate.

## Boulton — routine atomisation and chaining

[Routine atomisation](https://unstoppablelearning.substack.com/p/routine-atomisation);
[Why we sometimes need to chain atoms](https://eedi.substack.com/p/why-we-sometimes-need-to-chain-atoms).

**Establishes.** Atomisation is analysis, not teaching — reclassifying "steps
in a process" as mathematically meaningful atoms, each of a type that dictates
how it is communicated. Crucially: students who have mastered every atom in
isolation still collapse when asked to run the whole routine, because the jump
from atoms to assembly is itself a thing to be taught. Atoms must be chained in
stages.

**Licenses.** The existence of this skill as separate from `teaching-sequence`
— the assembly is its own teaching problem. Step 5's backward chain, and the
rule that every rung ends in an answer of the terminal type so no attempt
stalls halfway.

## Renkl — self-explanation

Covered in the Atkinson et al. review above.

**Establishes.** Learners who explain a worked example to themselves transfer
better than learners who study one. Prompts that ask *why* a line was done
outperform prompts that ask *what* was done.

**Licenses.** The single self-explanation prompt in Step 4, and its wording as
a why-question aimed at the distractor.
