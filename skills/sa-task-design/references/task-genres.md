# Task genres — the run protocol for each

Each entry: what it is, how long, how to run it minute by minute, the SA
disposition it develops, and how to *make* one when the bank has nothing.
The skill should be able to run any of these with no internet access.

---

## 1. Which One Doesn't Belong (WODB)
**Format.** Four things in a 2×2 grid. For every one of the four, there is a
reason it is the odd one out.
**Length.** 5–8 min. Starter or plenary.
**Run.** Show grid, 60 s silent thinking, "find a reason for the one you're most
sure about", pairs, then take the four in turn and insist on a reason for each.
The payoff is when the class finds the fourth reason.
**Disposition.** Reflective (every answer defensible; you must justify, not
guess). Capability: Reasoning.
**How to make one.** Pick a property with four levels. Choose four objects so
each is the unique one to break a different property. Test it: write the four
reasons down before you use it. If you can only find three, it isn't finished.
**Source.** wodb.ca (shapes, numbers, graphs, incomplete sets). Site is a thin
landing page; the sets live at `wodb.ca/shapes.html` etc. Easy to author your own.

## 2. Estimation (Estimation 180 style)
**Format.** A photo with a measurable quantity. Students give *too low*, *too
high*, then a best estimate with a reason.
**Length.** 5 min.
**Run.** Too low / too high first — this is what makes it safe; nobody is wrong
yet. Then the estimate and, critically, the *reason*. Reveal. Discuss whose
reasoning was best, not who was closest.
**Disposition.** Resilient (low-stakes entry, everyone commits), Resourceful
(choosing a benchmark).
**How to make one.** Any photo where a known object gives scale. Best sequences
run several days off the same photo family so the benchmark carries forward.
**Source.** estimation180.com — 300 days, grouped in 20s. Free, no login.

## 3. Same But Different / Alike and Different
**Format.** Two images, expressions or diagrams. "These are the same but they
are different — how?"
**Length.** 5–8 min.
**Run.** Show both. "Same but different." Take *sames* first (safer), then
*differents*. Push for mathematical rather than surface answers.
**Disposition.** Reflective. Capability: Developing understanding — this is the
genre for connecting representations.
**How to make one.** Take a worked example and change exactly one structural
feature. `3(x+2)` vs `3x+2`. A trapezium vs a parallelogram with equal area.
**Source.** samebutdifferentmath.com — organised by domain incl. Fractions/Ratios/
Percentages, Geometry, Algebra, High School Math. Free.

## 4. Open Middle
**Format.** A skeleton with boxes and a digit constraint, usually optimising.
*"Using the digits 1 to 9 at most once each, fill the boxes to make the result
as large as possible."*
**Length.** 8–15 min. Also the default *Spicy* / extension question.
**Run.** Everyone gets an answer within two minutes (any valid filling). Then
"can you beat it?" Board the best-so-far and keep raising it. Close by asking
*how do you know that's the best* — that's the mathematics.
**Disposition.** Resilient (there is always a next attempt), and Reasoning at the
close.
**How to make one.** Take the skill's standard form, blank the numbers, add
"digits 1–9 at most once each" and an optimisation. **Verify the optimum by
exhaustive search in code, never by intuition** — this is the single most common
way these go wrong.
**Source.** openmiddle.com, organised K–Calculus by topic. Free; trademark held
by Glenrock Consulting, no explicit reuse licence — link rather than reproduce
verbatim in shared material.

## 5. SSDD — Same Surface, Different Deep structure
**Format.** Four questions sharing one diagram/context/set of numbers, each
needing completely different mathematics.
**Length.** 10–15 min.
**Run.** All four visible at once. Students must *say which topic each is* before
solving. That naming step is the whole point.
**Disposition.** Resourceful (choosing the method is the task).
**Use it for.** The specific failure where students pick a method from surface
features — "there's a right angle so it's Pythagoras".
**Source.** ssddproblems.com (Craig Barton). Hundreds of sets, browsable grid, no
explicit licence stated. This school already has an `ssdd-build` skill — dispatch
to it rather than duplicating.

## 6. Maths Venns
**Format.** A two- or three-circle Venn with a criterion on each circle. Students
find an example for every region — including deciding which regions are empty.
**Length.** 10–20 min.
**Run.** Give the easy regions first as a way in. The real work is the
intersection and the *impossible* regions; "is it impossible or have we just not
found one?" is the reasoning payoff.
**Disposition.** Resourceful and Reasoning.
**How to make one.** Choose two properties that are independent-ish for the topic
(e.g. "has integer solutions" / "has a repeated root"). Check each region
yourself before use.
**Source.** **mathsvenns.com** (note the *s* — `mathvenns.com` does not resolve).
Craig Barton. Organised Number / Algebra / Geometry and Measures / Statistics and
Probability / A Level.

## 7. Slow reveal graphs
**Format.** A real graph revealed in stages — data first, then axes, then labels,
then title and source.
**Length.** 8–15 min.
**Run.** At each reveal: "what do you notice, what do you wonder, what's your
story now?" Students revise their interpretation aloud. Never reveal the title
early — the title kills the task.
**Disposition.** Reflective (public revision of a claim). Capability: Reasoning.
**Curriculum fit.** Years 9–10 Statistics, especially `AC9M10ST01` (claims,
inference, bias) and `AC9M9ST02` (representation supporting a point of view).
**Source.** slowrevealgraphs.com (Jenna Laib). Organised by graph type — bar,
line, scatterplot, histogram, boxplot — and by theme and grade band. Free.

## 8. Goal-free problems
**Format.** A standard exam diagram with the question removed. *"Here is the
diagram. Work out everything you can."*
**Length.** 5–10 min.
**Run.** Individually list every deducible quantity. Then compare lists. Then —
optionally — reveal the original question and ask which of their deductions was
needed.
**Why it works.** Removes means-end analysis, which is what eats working memory
in novices. Very strong for students who freeze at multi-step questions.
**Disposition.** Resourceful. Also the best low-prep genre in this list — any
past-paper diagram works.
**Source.** goalfreeproblems.blogspot.com (Peter Mattock) — 100 Foundation + 100
Higher, sorted Number / Algebra / Geometry and Measures / Data and Probability /
Ratio and Proportion / Mixed / A-Level.

## 9. Three-act mathematical modelling
**Format.** Act 1: a short video or image, no numbers, ending in an obvious
question. Act 2: students say what information they need; teacher supplies it.
Act 3: the answer is revealed, not told. Sequel: a harder version.
**Length.** 40–55 min. This is a whole-lesson task.
**Run.** Act 1 → "what do you notice / wonder" → agree the question → *students
estimate too-high/too-low first* → Act 2 information request → work → Act 3
reveal → discuss whose model was closest and why → sequel.
**Disposition.** All three, which is why it earns a whole lesson. Directly meets
the modelling descriptors (`AC9M7N09`, `AC9M8A03`, `AC9M9A05`, `AC9M10M05`) whose
wording — *formulate, choose a representation, interpret, review the model* — is
this lesson shape.
**Sources.** See `three-act-catalogue.md` for the full 83-task index with the
suggested question for each. Media at 101qs.com, gfletchy.com,
tapintoteenminds.com. Check your own drive first — see "Your own local library"
in `source-catalogue.md`.

## 10. Citizen Math (formerly Mathalicious)
**Format.** A two-day lesson built on a real social/consumer question, with a
student handout and a teacher guide.
**Length.** Designed for two lessons; the handout usually splits at a natural
point for one.
**Disposition.** Resourceful, and the "why are we learning this" answer.
If your school holds downloaded Citizen Math lessons, **prefer these over
anything web-hosted** — they are present, complete, and come with a teacher
guide carrying the expected student responses. See "Your own local library" in
`source-catalogue.md` for how to point this skill at them.

## 11. Thinking-classroom (BTC) tasks and thin slicing
**Format.** Two kinds get conflated. *Thinking tasks* are non-curricular or
curricular problems run at vertical non-permanent surfaces in visibly random
groups. *Thin slicing* is a sequence of questions each differing from the last by
one feature, worked at the boards.
**Length.** 20–55 min.
**Important.** Thin slicing overlaps almost exactly with what this pack calls
minimally different sequences — dispatch to `slop-build`, don't rebuild it.
**Disposition.** Resilient (the whole method is about not rescuing students too
early).
**Source.** The BTC K-12 curricular task sheet — see `btc-index.md`, including
how to re-pull it and the honest caveat about its contents.

## 12. Non-routine / competition problems
Dispatch to the existing `non-routine-build` skill. Sources worth naming that
aren't in it: **Problemo** (problemo.edu.au, Australian Maths Trust — 400+
problems years 3–10, classified by Australian Curriculum strand, *with enabling
and extending prompts*; free tier is 100 problems, paid from $9.99/mo) and
**MathPickle** (mathpickle.com, organised by grade, Creative Commons, explicitly
invites classroom use).

## 13. Error analysis / "find the mistake"
**Format.** A worked solution containing one specific error. "What did they do,
where did it go wrong, and what would you say to them?"
**Length.** 5–10 min.
**Run.** Do not say there is an error until they've read it. Ask "is this right?"
**Disposition.** Reflective. Best plenary genre in the list.
**How to make one.** Take the misconception already named in the atomisation for
that lesson and write the solution a student holding it would produce. The
distractor rationale from `teaching-sequence` is exactly the raw material.
**Source.** mathmistakes.org (Michael Pershan, running since 2012) collects real
student work; useful as a source of *authentic* errors rather than invented ones.

## 14. Visual patterns
**Format.** Steps 1, 2, 3 of a growing pattern. "How many at step 43? Write the
rule. Where do you see the rule in the picture?"
**Length.** 8–12 min.
**Run.** The third question is the one that matters — the algebra should be
*read off the picture*, giving different students genuinely different but
equivalent expressions. That equivalence is the discussion.
**Curriculum fit.** `AC9M7A05` (tables of values from visually growing patterns)
almost word for word.
**Source.** visualpatterns.org (Fawn Nguyen) — 100s of patterns, each labelled
with the count at step 43.

## 15. Estimation-adjacent starters worth knowing
- **Math Arguments 180** (matharguments180.blogspot.com) — 520+ numbered
  discussion prompts, label-based topic index. Format is a claim to argue about.
  Posts thin out after 2020 but the archive is intact.
- **Math for Love openers** (mathforlove.com/lessons/openers) — named routines
  including Broken Calculator, Counterexamples, Fill the Stairs, Target Number,
  Guess My Number, Fraction Talks, Unit Chats, 2-4-6 Puzzle, Horseshoes.
  Mostly primary-leaning; Broken Calculator, Target Number, Counterexamples and
  2-4-6 all work at years 7–9.
- **Play With Your Math** (playwithyourmath.com) — 32 numbered poster-format
  problems, one page each, deliberately no year label. Excellent Friday tasks.
